# Thesis and topic sentence: the sources

Gathered 15 October 2026. Each quotation comes from a copy saved in the Hands-On Linux Infrastructure repo under `rhythm/sources/`, with its page.

## 1. Alexander Bain, *English Composition and Rhetoric* (1866)

The first systematic rules for the paragraph. Copy: American edition, revised, D. Appleton, New York, 1867 (entered 1866). `sources/bain-1867-american-ed.txt`, from archive.org item `englishcompositi00bainuoft`.

- §158, p. 142: the paragraph "is a collection of sentences with unity of purpose." It "handles and exhausts a distinct topic."
- §176, p. 150, Rule III: "The opening sentence, unless so constructed as to be obviously preparatory, is expected to indicate with prominence the subject of the paragraph."
- §178, p. 151, Rule V: "The paragraph should possess unity; which implies a definite purpose, and forbids digressions and irrelevant matter." Bain adds that the rule "belongs to compositions that address the understanding, and is not strictly enforced in Poetry."

Bain never uses the words "topic sentence." He gives the rule; the name came later (D'Angelo, p. 431).

## 2. William Strunk Jr., *The Elements of Style* (1918; 1920 printing)

`sources/strunk-1920-gutenberg.txt`, Project Gutenberg 37134.

- Rule 8: "Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic."
- Rule 9: "As a rule, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end it in conformity with the beginning."
  - Why: "Again, the object is to aid the reader." It lets the reader "discover the purpose of each paragraph as he begins to read it, and to retain this purpose in mind as he ends it."
  - The most useful paragraph "in exposition and argument" has three parts: (a) the topic sentence "at or near the beginning"; (b) sentences that "explain or establish or develop" it; (c) a final sentence that "emphasizes the thought of the topic sentence or states some important consequence."
  - "Ending with a digression, or with an unimportant detail, is particularly to be avoided."

## 3. James McCrimmon, *Writing with a Purpose*, 3rd ed. (1963)

In `references/mccrimmon-1963-thesis.md`, p. 40. The thesis "is to the whole paper what the topic sentence or topic idea is to the paragraph." It must be "restricted, unified, and precise."

## 4. Frank J. D'Angelo, "The Topic Sentence Revisited," *College Composition and Communication* 37.4 (Dec. 1986), pp. 431–441

`sources/dangelo-1986-topic-sentence-revisited.pdf` (JSTOR 357913). A survey of the research up to 1986.

**What professional writers do (p. 435).** Richard Braddock's study, "The Frequency and Placement of Topic Sentences in Expository Prose" (*Research in the Teaching of English* 8.3, 1974), found that fewer than half of the paragraphs in his sample of professional essays had explicit topic sentences. Only 13% began with one. D'Angelo quotes Braddock's conclusion, which teachers often skip: the essays "would have been clearer and more comfortable to read if the paragraphs had presented more explicit topic sentences" (Braddock, p. 301).

**What readers do (pp. 435–437).** The reading studies he cites point the other way:
- Gagné and Wiegand: the topic sentence acts as an advance organizer and helps the reader remember the paragraph.
- Aulls: sixth graders who read paragraphs with topic sentences recalled more than those who read paragraphs without them.
- Richards, and Clements: readers remember more when the topic sentence comes first.
- Fishman: a topic sentence in first position simplifies the reader's work, because the reader knows the content in advance.
- Kieras: good paragraphs open with a topic sentence; good essays carry a global topic as well. Without them, readers take longer, recall less and miss the main ideas.
- Meyer and colleagues: ninth graders who used a text's top-level structure recalled more.

**His conclusion (pp. 438–439).** Writers who use topic sentences help readers "recall more of what they have read" and "read more efficiently (i.e., read faster)." He does not require one in every paragraph. He recommends them where the occasion warrants, "for example, in some kinds of expository writing whose aim is to give clear directions or advice for a general audience."

## 5. Francis Christensen, "Symposium on the Paragraph," *College Composition and Communication* 17.2 (May 1966), pp. 60–87

`sources/christensen-1966-symposium-ED021873.pdf`, ERIC ED 021 873, free. Christensen restates the theory of his October 1965 essay, "A Generative Rhetoric of the Paragraph," and answers Becker and Rodgers. His section opens on p. 60.

- p. 61, the old analogy "derived from Bain": "the topic sentence of a paragraph is to the supporting sentences what the subject of a sentence is to its predicate."
- p. 61, his own analogy: "the topic sentence of a paragraph is to the supporting sentences what the base clause of a cumulative sentence is to its free modifiers."
- p. 61, levels: "When analyzed for levels of structure, either a sentence or a paragraph may prove to have the form 1 2 2 2 etc. or 1 2 3 4 etc. or any combination of these coordinate and subordinate sequences."
  - 1 2 2 is the house model: a topic sentence at level 1, two supports at level 2, each a comment on the topic and equal to the other.
- p. 62, his four principles, the same for sentence and paragraph: addition; direction of movement; "levels of generality or abstraction"; and density of texture. He found them "by induction," but "they may be used normatively."
- p. 66, on studying what published writers do: "We are sure to find paragraphs that are mere huddles of sentences and complete pieces that are mere huddles of such paragraphs. We cannot base our teaching on such models."

The October 1965 essay itself (CCC 16.3, pp. 144–156) sits behind NCTE's paywall. JSTOR 355728.

## 6. Craig G. Smith, "Braddock Revisited," *The Reading Matrix* 8.1 (April 2008), pp. 78–95

`sources/smith-2008-braddock-revisited.pdf`, free from readingmatrix.com. A replication of Braddock.

- p. 82: Braddock's corpus held twenty-five essays from *The Atlantic*, *The New Yorker*, *The Reporter* and *Saturday Review*. Smith drew twenty-five from *American Heritage* and *American History Illustrated*.
- p. 85: Smith counted by "discourse block," a run of paragraphs under one topic. "66% contained the topic idea at the beginning."
- pp. 86–87: Braddock's own numbers, for paragraphs longer than four T-units (a T-unit is a main clause and what hangs on it): 62% had an explicit topic idea in the first or second T-unit.
- p. 89: "Braddock's finding, that only 13% of paragraphs in expository academic writing begin with topic sentences, is misleading." Short paragraphs often fall under a topic sentence in the paragraph before.
- Smith also cites Popken (1987), who studied seven fields, from literature to biochemistry, and found Braddock's single kind of essay "widely over-generalized" (p. 79).

**Braddock 1974 itself** (*Research in the Teaching of English* 8.3, pp. 287–302, ERIC EJ 115737) has no free copy. NCTE's site refused a download (HTTP 403). The figures here come from his own tables as D'Angelo and Smith quote them.

## What this means for the skill

1. The chain runs from Bain (1866) to Strunk (1918) to McCrimmon (1963): one topic per paragraph, the topic named first, the thesis doing for the whole piece what the topic sentence does for a paragraph.
2. Braddock shows that many published writers skip the topic sentence. The reading studies show that readers pay for it. The house rule rests on the reader, as Strunk's does: "the object is to aid the reader."
3. Bain allows one exception: an opening sentence "obviously preparatory." A scene before the thesis, as in the LUC articles, fits that exception. The check should allow it rather than flag it.
4. D'Angelo's example of where topic sentences matter most, directions and advice for a general reader, describes the book and the Substack pieces.

5. Christensen's levels give the check a way to read a paragraph: the topic sentence at level 1, each support at level 2. A sentence at level 1 in the middle of a paragraph starts a second topic.
6. Smith shows a topic sentence can govern a run of short paragraphs. The check should judge a block under one topic, not each short paragraph alone.
