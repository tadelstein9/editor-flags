# What a thesis sentence was in 1966: McCrimmon, *Writing with a Purpose*, 3rd ed. (1963)

James M. McCrimmon, *Writing with a Purpose*, third edition, Houghton Mifflin, Boston, 1963. Internet Archive, archive.org/details/bwb_Y0-BNH-654_3rded (lending copy), read through its search-inside service on 13 Oct 2026. The table of contents lists "purpose statement: the thesis (40)"; the scan's leaf numbers run ahead of the printed page numbers.

Section "Purpose Statements: The Thesis" (printed p. 40):

> "For many student essays the most useful — and the most exacting — kind of purpose statement is the thesis. A thesis is a single sentence, preferably a simple sentence, which expresses the basic idea the paper is to develop. The thesis is to the whole paper what the topic sentence or topic idea is to the paragraph."

> "A good thesis is restricted, unified, and precise. To be restricted, it must indicate which of several approaches to the subject the writer will take. It thus limits the scope of the paper to what can be properly discussed in the space available."

> "A good thesis is unified as well as restricted. To be unified, it must commit the writer to deal with only one dominant idea."

His example: "The United Nations Organization has major weaknesses and cannot prevent a major war" asks the writer to do two things. If the two are related, "he should show that relation in his thesis: 'The organization of the UN makes it incapable of preventing a war between major powers.'"

> "Finally, a thesis should be precise. It should be phrased in words which permit only one interpretation."

The book's reference chart lists the sequence a freshman worked through: purposeless writing, restriction, thesis, outline; unity, coherence, transitional markers, transitional paragraphs; subordination, parallel sentence, periodic sentence, emphasis, variety.

Not proven: that UT Austin's freshman course used this book in 1966. It was a leading freshman composition text of that period.
